Read it as the case against a subscription: at low consumption the margin looks high only because the customer is overpaying for very few tokens, and where usage fills the free bundle but the fee is low the margin turns deeply negative because Akamai still pays for those tokens. Recommendation

Launch on Model 1 as the single pricing model. Embed the markup in one clean all-in rate and cap each token type at the cost-basis partner's retail price, so Akamai never lists above what a customer could pay the partner directly. Do not launch Model 2 (uncapped) or Model 3 (subscription). One usage-based rate is the simplest thing to sell and it is fair at every usage level, from the smallest developer to the largest account.

The goal is adoption. Margin is a floor to protect, not the number to maximise. Revenue follows from developers, token growth and retention, so it is treated as an output rather than a target.

Three key reasons

1. Willingness to pay and market standing

Akamai resells a white-labelled model that performs the same as the partner. Without a hard inference-path differentiator such as edge security, a web application firewall, prompt-injection defense or PII handling, buyers will not pay a premium over the partner or the discount aggregators. Model 1 caps each token type at the partner's retail and passes the negotiated discount through, so Akamai sits at or below the field. The positioning maps show where Akamai lands for each model, and under Model 1 it stays left of the partner on price at equal performance.

2. Revenue and margin hold together

Model 1 earns a unit margin of markup divided by one plus markup. At the current markup of that is , and it does not move with the discount. A deeper partner discount therefore lowers the customer price and helps adoption without eroding margin. The forecast panel on the other tab shows the adoption ramp reaching an exit run-rate in the tens of millions by year 3, with EBITDA turning positive in year 2 on the default assumptions.

3. Brand, trust and simplicity

A single all-in card price with the markup embedded reads as simple and honest, with no separate fee line to defend. Capping at the partner's retail signals fair pricing to technical buyers who can check the market in one click. The durable advantages are enterprise trust, an integrated cloud with data locality, one bill and no egress charges, and a global day-one launch. These earn the customer's choice rather than a per-token premium.

Why not the subscription or the uncapped rate

Model 3, subscription. Not recommended. A subscription only creates value when usage is high, because the effective price is the fee divided by the tokens actually used. A light user who consumes a fraction of the bundle pays a very high price per million tokens, so low-usage cohorts overpay and churn or never convert. Worked example: a $20 fee over 10M tokens used is $2.00 per 1M, against $0.20 per 1M if the full 100M bundle were consumed, a tenfold penalty on the light user. The subscription margin heatmap on the interactive tab shows the same instability from Akamai's side, swinging from deeply negative where a low fee has to cover a filled free bundle to very high where the customer is overpaying. A single usage-based rate avoids all of this and scales cleanly across every cohort.

Model 2, uncapped. Not recommended. Without a ceiling it can list above the partner once the markup passes discount divided by one minus discount, which undercuts the adoption thesis. Revisit only if a hard differentiator ever justifies a premium.
